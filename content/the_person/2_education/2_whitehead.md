title: Whitehead and the world as process

During the pandemic I came across Alfred North Whitehead through a geophysicist friend. Whitehead was a mathematician who became a philosopher of nature. He warned against the *fallacy of misplaced concreteness*: mistaking our abstractions for the world itself. As a climate scientist I work with abstractions all the time (indices, modes, models), and Whitehead keeps reminding me that they are tools for thinking about a world made of processes and relations, not the world itself.

Whitehead also made me think about causality for the first time. As the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy explains, Whitehead held that perception is a symbolic interplay between two pure modes. The first is pure sense perception, which he eventually called "perception in the mode of presentational immediacy". The second is a more basic perception of causal relatedness, which he called "perception in the mode of causal efficacy". Considering perception as a whole, rather than pure sense perception alone, means working with all perceptual data and not just Hume's sense data. For Whitehead, this also brings in the other half of the evidence: our intuitions of the relatedness of nature, of "the togetherness of things". As he put it:

> The togetherness of things involves some doctrine of mutual immanence. In some sense or other … each happening is a factor in the nature of every other happening. (*Nature and Life*, 1934)

Of course, I understood very little of this when I read it. I let it in and let it operate on me, the way very deep ideas work: in mysterious ways.

How does this apply to what I study? Do we have a language for causality in climate science? I'm honestly still struggling to find the right language for it. In physics or geophysics, we learn how to infer causality through experiments. When we study more complex macroscale systems, like fluids or atmospheric dynamics, we learn to use budget equations or wave solutions that more or less help us interpret maps and correlations. We can infer causality from model simulations, but then we have to really understand and trust our models. We don't learn how to infer causality from observations of a complex system that we cannot experiment with. This always puzzled me, and it is what guided my choice of postdoc.
